Chapter 2 of Joseph Spencer's For Zion is titled "Faith and Hope," and it's a close reading of Romans 4

Spencer's framework maps well onto the AI debate, because most reactions to AI in schools fall into his two failure modes.

**Desperation: trying to save the old system with its own tools.** This is the scramble for AI detectors, lockdown browsers, and ever-cleverer assignment rules. It assumes the current way of doing school still works and we just need to find the right patch. Spencer would say this is "objectively hopeful, subjectively hopeless": it believes a fix exists within the old order but feels constantly outrun. The mirror image is edtech boosterism, which also stays inside the old order, just with confidence instead of panic. It's the "ordinary hope" he calls self-oriented: faith in our own mastery of the tools.

**Despair: deciding it's over.** "The essay is dead," "students will never think for themselves again," "why teach writing at all?" Despair sees clearly that the old scheme can't survive, which is why Spencer puts it closer to hope. But it concludes nothing can be done.

**Hope: letting go of the old scheme joyfully.** Real hope admits that some things really are passing away: the take-home essay as proof of learning, homework as a measure of effort, the teacher as the sole gatekeeper of information. It doesn't mourn them or cling to them. It sees the disruption as an opening to rebuild learning on better foundations: more dialogue, oral defense, apprenticeship, visible thinking, work done in community rather than in isolation.

**Faith anchors that hope.** For Spencer, hope needs trust in something prior that doesn't itself get disrupted. Otherwise, "rebuilding" is just another self-confident project. For a teacher, that anchor might be the conviction that learning is about forming persons, not producing outputs. In a Latter-day Saint setting, it could be scriptures like "the glory of God is intelligence" (D&C 93:36) or seeking learning "by study and also by faith" (D&C 88:118). Faith keeps hope oriented toward what education is *for*. Hope, in turn, gives that faith traction: it's not enough to believe education matters; you have to trust it can actually be reordered around that purpose.

**One caution for the analogy:** Spencer's disrupting event is the resurrection, a divine act. AI is a human technology, not a revelation. The parallel works best if AI is treated as the thing that *exposes* the old order's fragility, not as the saving event itself. Otherwise you slide back into boosterism.

These pages sharpen the argument quite a bit. The biggest change: **desperation, not boosterism, is the main counterfeit.** Here's the revised mapping.

**Desperation: the hunt for the procedure.** Spencer says desperation believes there's a *way* (a sequence, a procedure) out of the problem, and that the only obstacle is my own inadequacy. That's most of the current AI conversation in schools: the right detector, the right policy, the AI-proof assignment, the perfect integration framework. The subtext is "a fix exists and I haven't found it yet," so teachers push themselves past their limits and burn out. His image fits perfectly: the hungry sleeper dreaming of food (Isa. 29:8). Anti-AI crackdowns and frantic AI adoption can both be desperation, because both assume the old system can be saved with the right technique.

**Despair: honestly admitting the old way can't be saved.** Spencer insists hope only breaks at the point of real impossibility. For educators, that means saying plainly that the take-home essay can no longer verify who did the thinking, and no procedure will restore it. That admission isn't the enemy of hope; it's the doorway.

**Hope: "I am not in possibility's way."** This is the most freeing line for teachers. Once you see there's no hidden procedure you're failing to find, you can relax. The old scheme passing away isn't your fault, and its future doesn't rest on your cleverness. That's the real sense of AI "exposing the old order": it shows that the order was already passing away (1 Cor. 7:31). The Bloch line you highlighted fits well: education is process, with an unfinished past and a possible future, not a fixed system to defend.

**Hope as God wants, not as I want.** The line you underlined is the check on everything. Ordinary hope wants *my* classroom preserved, *my* expertise honored, *my* school seen as innovative. Virtuous hope surrenders that and asks what God intends learning to become.

**Faith as the anchor.** For Spencer, faith insists on a past revelatory event that contains a promise that things can be different. For Latter-day Saint educators, that anchor could be revealed promises about learning: that intelligence rises with us in the resurrection (D&C 130:18), or seeking learning by study and by faith (D&C 88:118). Those promises, not the technology, are what hope draws on.

**A bonus parallel from the chapter's opening:** Paul argues faith *upholds* the Law rather than overthrowing it (Rom. 3:31), finding his account inside the Law itself. Likewise, a hopeful response to AI doesn't abandon education's tradition. It rediscovers older forms already in it: dialogue, mentorship, oral examination, apprenticeship.

### Slides

# Hopeless but Hoping
### Joseph Spencer's *For Zion* (ch. 2) and AI in Education

---

## Slide 1: Upholding, Not Overthrowing

> "…it is not enough simply to state that Christian faith upholds rather than overthrows the Law."

**AI application**
- Paul finds his new account of faith *inside* the old Law.
- A hopeful response to AI doesn't abandon education's tradition. It recovers older forms already in it: dialogue, mentorship, oral examination, apprenticeship.

---

## Slide 2: The Key Phrase

> "Hopeless but hoping…"

**AI application**
- Paul's phrase joins two opposites: "beyond hope" and "resting on hope."
- Education now faces both at once: the old model is beyond saving, and yet something better is possible.

---

## Slide 3: Faith Looks Back

> "…a kind of militant insistence that a past revelatory event took place…"

**AI application**
- Hope needs an anchor that the disruption doesn't touch.
- For Latter-day Saint educators: revealed promises about learning (D&C 88:118; D&C 130:18).
- The anchor is the promise, not the technology.

---

## Slide 4: A Promise of Difference

> "…a *promise*, an indication that *things can be different*."

**AI application**
- Faith in education's purpose isn't nostalgia for how school used to work.
- It's trust that learning can take new and better forms.

---

## Slide 5: The Old Order Is Passing

> "…the present scheme of things is passing away" (1 Cor. 7:31)

**AI application**
- AI didn't break the system; it **exposed** what was already fragile.
- The take-home essay as proof of thinking, homework as proof of effort, the teacher as gatekeeper of information: these were already passing.

---

## Slide 6: Education as Process

> "…present, unfinished past, and above all: possible future."
> (Ernst Bloch, quoted by Spencer)

**AI application**
- Education is not a fixed system to defend but an unfinished process.
- The question isn't "How do we protect school?" but "What can learning become?"

---

## Slide 7: Two Kinds of Hope

> "The genuine hoper does not hope that things will turn out as *she* wants them to be, but as *God* wants them to be."

**AI application**
- Ordinary hope: *my* classroom preserved, *my* expertise honored, *my* school seen as innovative.
- Virtuous hope: asking what God intends learning to become.

---

## Slide 8: The Counterfeit

> "…desperation is the counterfeit of genuine hope…"

**AI application**
- The biggest danger isn't despair about AI. It's desperation that *looks* like hope.
- Frantic crackdowns and frantic adoption can both be counterfeits.

---

## Slide 9: The Hunt for the Procedure

> "…there *is* some way out of the situation but that *I* cannot find it."

**AI application**
- The right detector, the right policy, the AI-proof assignment, the perfect framework.
- Desperation assumes the fix exists and blames teachers for not finding it.
- Result: educators pushing past their limits and burning out.

---

## Slide 10: The Hungry Dreamer

> "…the hungry person dreams of eating but wakes up still hungry" (Isa. 29:8)

**AI application**
- Each new tool or policy promises to restore the old order.
- We wake up still hungry, because the procedure we're dreaming of doesn't exist.

---

## Slide 11: Where Hope Breaks In

> "…it is with hopelessness or despair that hope breaks."

**AI application**
- Honest admission comes first: the old model can't verify who did the thinking, and no procedure will restore it.
- That admission isn't defeat. It's the doorway.

---

## Slide 12: Out of Possibility's Way

> "…*I am not in possibility's way*."

**AI application**
- Teachers can relax: the old scheme passing isn't their failure.
- The future of learning doesn't rest on anyone's cleverness.

---

## Slide 13: Realistic Hope

> "Hope is thus, unlike self-deluding desperation, profoundly realistic…"

**AI application**
- Hope doesn't deny AI's disruption or pretend nothing changed.
- It sees the situation clearly and still expects something better.

---

## Slide 14: Miserable vs. Joyful

> "…hope gives up on the world in joyful affirmation that it can—and *will*—be otherwise."

**AI application**
- Despair and hope both let go of the old model.
- Despair lets go miserably ("school is dead"); hope lets go joyfully ("learning can be reborn").

---

## Slide 15: Getting to Work

> "…one garners the hope necessary to get to work on the world."

**AI application**
- Hope isn't passive waiting. It's what makes rebuilding possible.
- (Chapter 3's "subjective hopefulness" can supply concrete next steps.)

---

## Slide 16: Closing

> "Hope gives faith to be genuinely faithful precisely as faith gives hope to be genuinely hopeful."

**AI application**
- Faith keeps our hope about AI anchored in what education is *for*.
- Hope keeps that faith from becoming mere nostalgia, turning it into trust that learning can truly be reordered.
