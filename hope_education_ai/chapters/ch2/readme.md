# Chapter 2: Faith and Hope

Joseph M. Spencer, *For Zion: A Mormon Theology of Hope*, chapter 2.

## Summary

Spencer reads Romans 4:18–22 as an account of Abraham being “hopeless but hoping.” Abraham’s hope does not ignore the impossibility of his situation. Instead, it gives his faith consistency by drawing the future implications of God’s past promise. Faith remembers the revelatory event; hope trusts that the event can reorder what presently appears possible.

The chapter distinguishes genuine hope from desperation. Desperation remains objectively hopeful because it assumes that a path out already exists, but it is subjectively hopeless because the individual believes personal weakness prevents finding that path. Genuine hope begins when the situation is recognized as objectively impossible. At that point, hope no longer depends on discovering a hidden solution within the present order.

This makes Christian hope realistic and world-transforming. It accepts that the present “scheme of things” is passing away and trusts that the world can become otherwise because of Christ’s death and resurrection. Faith orients hope toward the world’s limits and their transformation; hope anchors faith by turning belief in God’s promise into work on the world.

## Argument at a glance

- Abraham’s hope strengthens his faith rather than replacing it.
- Faith is fidelity to a past revelatory event; hope draws out that event’s implications for the future.
- Desperation assumes a solution exists but treats personal inadequacy as the obstacle.
- Genuine hope appears at the point of objective impossibility.
- Hope trusts that the world’s present order can be transformed and therefore gets to work on the world.
